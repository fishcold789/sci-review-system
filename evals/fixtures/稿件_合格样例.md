Amplitude sizing rests on a comparison against a machined reference reflector: the calibration block must match the component in material, surface finish and attenuation [Felice2018]. Two consequences follow. The reflector is a flat-bottomed hole, so the reported quantity is an equivalent size and not a physical extent, and the reading is a lower bound rather than an upper one [Krautkramer1959]. Fitness-for-service needs the opposite, because what has to be demonstrated is that the flaw does not exceed a stated through-wall extent.

Curvature was handled by a single scalar offset in the original charts [Krautkramer1959]. That remedy assumed a known, constant and single radius. Conformal contact removes all three at once.

The quantity to be corrected has therefore gained a dimension at each step, from a scalar offset to an axial distribution and then to a field defined element by element, and no amount of recalibration recovers a single number that a chart can hold; a flexible array reading cannot separate a larger flaw from a poorer fit, so the pipeline industry moved to time-of-flight diffraction instead [Silk1975].
